# DAY 77 (cont.) — THE CARRIAGE GAP, AND NOBODY HAS EVER THOUGHT ABOUT OSK

**Dave accepts every word of Wend's list answer, drops the hurry** — *"on day 83 we can
send all three carts south if we need them"* — **and then asks three things she was not
expecting, one of which she has an answer to and one of which she has none.**

## ⚠ THE CARRIAGE GAP — d6 = 6, SHE HAD ALREADY FOUND IT AND HAD NOT RAISED IT

**Found during her week on the articles.** She had not volunteered it because *it is
not the venture's money and it is not her gesture to criticise.* **Dave asking is what
opens the page.**

> *"You asked the right question and you're going to like the answer less than you
> think."*

### CARRIAGE FOLLOWS WEIGHT, NOT VALUE — and the iron is the heaviest thing they own

| the Ludbury return load, day 25-27 | |
|---|---|
| Total | **718 gp of goods, ~1,170 lb, three carts** |
| **Iron, nails and salt** | **100 gp — and 600 lb** |
| **So the iron was** | **14% of the VALUE and 51% of the WEIGHT** |

**Trip costs off her own book:** cart wear **17 apiece × 3 = 51** · gate duty **~33** ·
**two of the three carts hired** (Osk's and Tam's) · road costs for fourteen people.
*(The foreigner's 10% was on the SELLING, not the carrying — it does not touch this.)*

**Carriage on that return load ≈ 100-140 gp. The iron's half of it: 50-70 gp** *(d6=5,
lands high).*

> ### **"SO IT COST YOU A HUNDRED AT LUDBURY AND ABOUT A HUNDRED AND SIXTY BY THE TIME IT STOOD IN WETHERSBY. AND YOU SOLD IT FOR A HUNDRED."**
>
> *"That wasn't at cost. **That was the biggest present anybody in this hundred has
> been given** — bigger than Bern's tools, and the only one where nobody knew the size
> of it. **Including the people who got it.**"*

**AND THE TRADE LESSON THAT EXPLAINS WHY THE GESTURE WORKED:** *"iron's dear this far
from a forge because of the ROAD, not because of the iron. That's why there was none.
You didn't undercut a smith. **You paid the road for them.**"*

### HER ANSWER — YES, with one correction and one warning (d6 = 4)

| | |
|---|---|
| **1 · "COST" MEANS LAID-DOWN COST** | purchase, duty, wages, wear. *"Anything short of that isn't a low price, it's a hole in your own book, and in a year you'd be telling me iron doesn't pay when what doesn't pay is not counting."* |
| **2 · BUT WAGES BELONG TO THE RUN, NOT TO THE IRON** | *"If Halm carts iron down and cloth back, his wage is the trip's, not the iron's. Hang the whole of it on the iron and the iron looks bad and the cloth looks a marvel — **and then somebody makes a decision off that, and the decision's wrong.** Split it by the weight, same as the wear."* **This is the correct accounting call and it is her standing crusade** |
| **3 · ⚠ AND THE DAY-59 FINDING RUNS BACKWARDS HERE** | **They cannot quietly reprice the old iron at 160.** *"They'd not see you correcting an error. They'd see you putting your price up half again the moment they got used to you."* |

> ### HER FIX: DON'T REPRICE THE OLD. PRICE THE NEW PROPERLY — AND SHOW THE ROAD IN THE PRICE
>
> *"Two numbers on the board. **What it cost at Ludbury, and what it cost to fetch.**
> Then it's not a price rise, it's an education, and the next one's legible before you
> make it."*
>
> **Her day-20 rule survives intact — *open-handed where it is FINITE and where it
> SHOWS*.** The first hundredweight was the gift; it was finite; it landed. **A
> standing line gets a standing price.**

## OSK — d6 = 2, AND SHE HAS NO VIEW AT ALL

**The first question all morning she does not reach for.**

> *"I'm a wool trader. I have never hired a carrier who wasn't Halm and I have **never
> once** thought about what one carrier owes another on his own road. I'd be guessing,
> and you'd go and act on it."*

**She names the right man — HALM — and the trap in the same breath:** you cannot ask a
carrier whether to cut his competitor in.

> *"So don't ask him about Osk. **Ask him what's DONE** — what does a carrier owe
> another carrier whose road he starts working? That he can answer without it being
> about him."*

**⚠ SAME INSTRUMENT AS OTTIR ON THE ROOF, ONE DAY APART: not *whose is it* / *should
we*, but *what is the CUSTOM*.** Two advisers, no contact, same move. **Worth noticing
as a pattern — in this hundred, custom is the cheap way to get an answer that nobody
can write a letter about.**

**The one thing she will say, from her own trade:**

> *"He told this market about your patrol with nobody asking him to, and he named you.
> In a town this size **a carrier is the news.** Whatever you settle — **he does not
> learn it from somebody else.**"*

### ⚠ AND THE RECORD ANSWERS THIS BETTER THAN SHE DOES — the party heard it themselves

**ANCEL, the outside buyer come north on a rumour, asked OSK in front of the party:**
***"What about your wool? Next summer. WHO DO I SPEAK TO?"*** **Never answered** — the
first outside buyer to ask that in this hundred in two years, and *he will not sit in
the square forever.*

**THERE IS NOW AN ANSWER TO THAT QUESTION.** Wend's structure is signed and the wool
operation is on the road. **And Osk runs Wethersby↔Marchford every fortnight and hands
off to PELL, who goes to LUDBURY — while TOLLER wants SIX CORRESPONDENTS IN SIX
TOWNS.**

> **A man who drives the same road every fortnight is not a cart. He is the POST.**

**OSK IS OUT ON HIS RUN (d6 = 3) — back about the 80th or 81st, ahead of the wool
party.**

| **down** | cloth to sell · Toller's goods · flax, next year |

| **up** | iron · salt · shears · return loads · **letters** |

> *"One load's a load. What I'd want, someday, is a **RUN.** Osk's got a run. **He

unmet.")*
