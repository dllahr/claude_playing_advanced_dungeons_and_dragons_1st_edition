# The wool venture — agreed day 12, Wethersby

**The party's first commercial undertaking.** Everything here was played at the
table. **It goes on paper in the fiction too**, at Wend's insistence and for a
reason that matters more than the money — see below.

## Why it exists

The wool staple moved. The buyer who came up every summer for twelve years came
late two years ago and not at all last year. So the clip did not sell, so the
flocks were sold off cheap *to each other*, so six holdings shed their hands at
Michaelmas — **twenty-odd people, all at once, for one reason.** Hask's four and
Coll's four are downstream of that, months apart.

**This summer's clip is still sitting in barns.** Wool keeps.

## The structure

**BUY, do not lend.** Wend's insistence, and she is right: a creditor of men who
cannot sell is holding paper off the insolvent, and they will hate him for it
inside a season. Buying means they have money today and owe nothing, and if there
is no market it is **the party's** loss and not theirs.

**Every seller chooses:**

- **the floor price and a SHARE of any eventual profit**, or
- **a good price and no share**

**d6 = 6 — all six holders took the SHARE.** Which means the party pays the
**floor**, not the premium Dave wanted to pay, and every wool-holder in the
hundred is now invested in the party's success.

### CAPITAL RETURNS FIRST

Wend's professional point and the one that protects the party: **the 450 gp comes
back off the top before anything is divided.** Otherwise a shareholder profits
while the party is still down on the deal. Profit is what remains after that.

## IT MUST BE IN WRITING — and this is not bookkeeping

Wend's own words:

> *"You're about to stand in six barns and promise six men a share of money that
> doesn't exist yet. There's a man in this room who gave eleven years on a promise
> made out loud in a yard and never written down. If you make that promise and you
> can't keep it, you will not be a man whose venture failed. **You'll be Rell's
> brother.** And there is no coming back from that in a hundred this size."*

**Every name, every share, every fleece by weight. Signed. Witnessed by Ottir. A
copy left in Wethersby while the party is away.** Wend writes it; she can write.

## The work it creates — which was the point

Carting in from six holdings. **Sorting** (fleece, belly wool and daggings do not
fetch the same). **Packing** into sacks. A dry store off the ground. **Guarding**
— four hundred gold of anything in a barn is a temptation.

*"That's a fortnight's work for as many hands as you've got. And every holder you
pay can put his own people back on. That's not four men. **That's most of
twenty.**"*

## MARROW SIGNED — day 16. All six holders in

He held out (d6=6 snag, d6=1 Marrow) with **very nearly half the district's clip**
in his barn (d6=5) — two years of fleece to the rafters. Wend's earlier read that
all six would take the share was wrong and **she owned it**: he would have signed
a week ago, then watched five carts go up to her barn and did the sum.

**He did not want money** (d6=5). He refused over the floor and he refused two
shares.

**He wanted his son placed.** **Sim, sixteen.** Apprenticed to any trade with a
roof on it — *"joiner, smith, cooper, saddler, I don't care"* — and he will pay
the premium over time out of the wool. **Signed at the floor, same as everyone.**

**Why he would not say it to Wend:** she knew the boy's mother. *"She'd have
something kind to say and I'd not have got through it."* Dave read that, said
nothing about it in front of her, asked her to step out, and told her exactly why
— she went **flat but not wounded (53)** and had genuinely not guessed (d6=5).

**Marrow's reasoning, and it is the district's epitaph in one man's mouth:**
sixty-four, biggest flock in the hundred, two years unsold, no word from Draycot.
*"I don't believe it comes back… in ten years there'll be sheep on that hill and
nobody left to shear them. And I'll not hand that to him and call it an
inheritance."*

## THE DIARY DEAL — Toller, day 16. A SEPARATE venture, funding this one

**Toller advised against carrying it to Ludbury**, against his own short-term
interest: *"You'd be selling cold, in a strange town, as a stranger passing
through… he knows within a minute you're not from Ludbury, you've got carts
outside, and you're leaving."* Ludbury **will** have a buyer (d6 = 4 — a staple
town has rich men who want things on shelves) but **Toller knows nobody there** and
can give no name. A hurried sale there fetches **80-90**, barely more than his own
standing offer, after five days of road.

**He offered 60 gp as a pawn.** Dave counter-offered *worse for the party and
better for Toller*: **30 gp now, Toller keeps the book, sells it at his own pace
and in his own way, and returns half the profit.**

**And Toller would not shake on it** — reaction 49, and the reason is the best
piece of commercial advice in the campaign:

> *"Half of WHAT profit? Say I sell it for two hundred. Less the thirty I gave
> you? Less the thirty and the four gold I paid a man to carry it to Marchford
> twice looking for a buyer? Less what I'd have paid you outright, which is
> seventy-eight, because in my head I've owned this book since Tuesday? **Those
> are four different numbers. All four are honest.** And in eight months one of us
> will feel robbed, over money neither of us has yet."*

### AGREED TERMS

- **30 gp advanced now.** Toller holds the diary.
- **Profit = sale price, less the 30, less any cost he can show a receipt or a
  name for.** No notional profit, no what-I'd-have-paid.
- **Halved.**
- **A year on it.** If unsold in twelve months they sit down again, *"because a
  book nobody's bought in a year isn't worth what I told you it was and we'd both
  need to know that."*
- **Written by Wend.**

**Funds ~174 -> ~204 gp.**

### Borrow or buy? Wend says BUY

- **d6 = 3 — the holders could spare one or two, and it would pinch them.** They
  would say yes because they hold shares. *"On the first turn of a partnership you
  have just signed on paper, that is a bad first favour to ask."* And a borrowed
  cart with a cracked axle means owing Marrow a better cart than he lent.
- **d6 = 1 — a sound cart resells at two-thirds of new.** 50 to buy, ~33 back. **A
  cart used once and sold costs 17 gp** — cheaper than the goodwill.
- **But do not sell them.** *"If this works you do it again next summer, and the
  summer after. You'll want your own carts."*

### THE RETURN LOAD — Wend's real point, and nobody had thought of it

> *"You're going down with three carts full and coming back with three carts
> empty. That's not a caravan, that's a delivery. **A trader buys at the far
> end.**"*

Ludbury is a staple town with everything this hundred has not seen in two years:
**iron, nails, tools, good cloth** (which Wend has been buying off pedlars at
robbery prices because there is no other way to get it here), **salt.** The party
will be standing in that market with the wool money in hand and three empty carts.

**This is the second profit and no one in Wethersby has made it in two years,
because nobody has had a cart going that way.**

## THE ACCOUNTING, settled day 16 — Wend's structure, agreed

### Costs come off the top, WITH the capital — but "cost" is defined

*"You can't carry every cost alone and share every gain — that's not a
partnership, that's a subscription."* Fodder, road food, wages, tolls: **off the
top**, before shares.

**THE CARTS ARE NOT A COST.** Wend's sharpest point, and she cited Toller's
four-honest-numbers lesson from the same morning:

> *"A cart's not spent. You buy it for fifty and you've still got it in November,
> worth three-and-thirty. Charge this venture a hundred gold for two carts you own
> free and clear and you're taking a hundred out of Marrow's share to buy yourself
> carts. **Charge it the wear.** Fifty out, three-and-thirty back — the venture
> bears **seventeen** a cart. You don't charge the harvest for the plough; you
> charge it for the wear on the plough."*

### The return goods are NOT in the shared pool — d6 = 5, Wend argued against

The holders put in **fleeces**. The return trade is the party's money, the party's
judgement, the party's risk, in a market no holder has seen. *"They can win and
they cannot lose. That's already an odd shape and it's an odd shape you chose.
Every extra pot you tip them into stretches it further."*

And the year-two trap: *"Once you've given it, it's theirs. The year you don't do
it, you're the man who took something away."*

**She granted the counter-argument honestly** — the carts are only empty because
the wool filled them going down — and still said no.

**Her alternative, adopted: be open-handed where it is FINITE and where it SHOWS.**
Sell the returned iron and nails to the district **at cost**, with the price
visible. A kindness they can hold, of a known amount, that does not compound.

### What the sale started

The venture went down as a wool trade and is coming back as a **cloth** one. The
loom is on a cart, **Ede the spinner comes north on a wage in a month**, the
weaver at **Lyde** will not move but might take a lad or two, and **Fenn's flax**
means a second fibre that is not sheep and does not fail in the same year.

Dave's rambling line on day 16 — *"if we had weavers in town we could sell cloth
instead of wool"* — is now a purchase, a payroll and a journey. **Wend runs it on
a wage and has said plainly she has never worked a loom.**

## THE POOL — day 32. The party gives up being the only buyer

**Wend is FACTOR for the hundred.** A shilling in the pound, paid by the holders,
**nothing from the party.** The pool is theirs, not the party's, and any holder
may join or sell elsewhere and is to be told so plainly.

**She argued for this against her own interest** and refused to accept it until
Dave counted the cost aloud: the clip cost **450** as sole buyer and would have
cost **~600** with one other man stood in Marrow's barn. **~150 gp off the top,
every summer, forever** — and Ancel is one competitor, not the last.

Her reason: as the party's partner *and* the holders' agent she would be on both
sides of the same fleece. *"I'd do it honestly for about a year and then there'd
be one bad summer, and somebody would ask which side I'd been on, and the honest
answer would be 'both', and that's the end of me in this town."*

**Accepted, on condition of the SUCCESSION document** — see `campaign/articles.md`.

## THE SHEARS — sell now. Hask's call

**Wend found the problem: nobody shears in October.** Thirty pair of steel into a
district that does not want them for seven months; Fenn is a flax man and never
had to think about *when*. And the one fortnight they could move is **the
fortnight six men are handed 38 gp each** — which she flagged herself rather than
let it be discovered.

**Hask, whose 19 gp is in that line, was given the decision and reversed her:**

> *"October is the only month in seven years that man can buy a decent pair of
> shears. Come May he shears with the pair he's got, it tears the fleece, and he
> loses on the clip — **your** clip — and makes do another year. You are not
> selling him a thing he doesn't want."*

**Sell now, at the honest price, and say aloud that it is the same price in May
so nobody thinks he was rushed.** Wend conceded and wrote it down.
